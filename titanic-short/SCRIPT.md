# "The Key That Sank the Titanic" — original script v1 (2026-11-17)
Format: cartoon-3d-short skill (~65 s, ~3.8 words/s), our own characters + story, no captions.

Facts (sources: Wikipedia "Titanic", "Sinking of the Titanic", "David Blair (sailor)", BBC News 2007-08-30
"Titanic key sold at auction", US Senate inquiry testimony of Frederick Fleet, SOLAS 1914):
- RMS Titanic left Southampton 10 April 1912 on her maiden voyage; widely called "unsinkable" in the press.
- Second Officer David Blair was removed from the crew in Southampton days before sailing (Lightoller replaced the
  reshuffle) and left with a key believed to open the locker holding the crow's nest binoculars.
- Lookouts Frederick Fleet and Reginald Lee had no binoculars. Night of 14 April: moonless, flat calm, so no surf at
  the iceberg's base. Fleet saw the berg ~37 s before impact, rang the bell three times and phoned the bridge:
  "Iceberg, right ahead!". Fleet later testified binoculars would have let them see it in time to get out of the way.
- Order "hard-a-starboard" swung the bow to port (left); the ship scraped the berg along her starboard side.
- 16 watertight compartments; she could stay afloat with the first four flooded; the damage opened five. The bulkheads
  only reached E deck, so water spilled over from one to the next as the bow went down. Many naval architects argue a
  head-on hit would have flooded fewer compartments and she would likely have stayed afloat.
- Sank 2:20 am, 15 April, 2 h 40 min after the collision; more than 1,500 people died.
- SOLAS 1914: lifeboats for everyone aboard, 24-hour radio watch.
- Blair's key sold at auction (Henry Aldridge & Son) in 2007 for £90,000.

## Script
The Titanic might have sunk because of one missing key.
It's April nineteen-twelve, and the biggest ship in the world is setting off on its first trip, with everyone calling it unsinkable.
But days before, an officer named David Blair got bumped off the crew, and walked off with a key still in his pocket.
That key most likely opened the locker with the lookouts' binoculars.
So four nights later, two lookouts are up in the crow's nest, squinting into a freezing, moonless night.
The sea is so flat, there are no waves breaking around the iceberg to give it away.
When they finally spot it, the ship is thirty-seven seconds away.
Three rings of the bell. Iceberg, right ahead!
The ship swings left, almost makes it, and scrapes along the ice instead.
Luckily, it didn't hit head-on.
Unluckily, it didn't hit head-on.
You see, Titanic was split into sixteen watertight rooms, and it could float with four of them flooded. The scrape opened five.
And the walls between them didn't go all the way up.
Ever tipped an ice cube tray? Fill one end, and the water spills into the next square, and the next.
Less than three hours later, the unsinkable ship was gone, and more than fifteen hundred people with it.
After that, every ship had to carry lifeboats for everyone, and keep its radio on all night.
And that key? In two thousand seven, it sold at auction for ninety thousand pounds.
Not bad, for a key that never even made the trip.
